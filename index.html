Byte & Brew

Where Great Coffee Meets Great Tech.

4 weeks
UX Research · UX Design · UI Design · Prototyping
Figma, FigJam, Canva, Notion, Claude

01 / Project Overview

Byte & Brew is a café concept for IT professionals, remote workers, freelancers, and students who need more than a place to enjoy coffee. It combines a specialty café, a productive workspace, and on-demand technical support.

The mobile app lets users reserve workspaces, order food and beverages, request assistance for workstation-related issues, and manage their visits. The aim is an environment where productivity and comfort coexist.

Specialty café

Premium coffee and food to support a comfortable visit.

Productive workspace

Dedicated spaces with work-friendly amenities.

Technical support

On-demand help for workstation-related issues.

02 / Problem & Solutions

Traditional cafés do not always support extended work sessions. The project brief identifies five obstacles and the services designed to address them.

ProblemProposed solution
Uncertain seating during busy hoursReserve a table or dedicated workspace before arriving.
Queues interrupt work sessionsOrder food and beverages through the app.
Limited power outlets and work-friendly seatingOffer dedicated workspaces with reliable amenities.
Technical issues disrupt productivityRequest workstation-related technical assistance.
Services require separate interactionsBring reservations, ordering, and support into one app.

03 / Design Process

I use an AI-assisted design workflow to explore research questions, develop ideas, and support iteration, while applying my own judgment to the design decisions.

Proposed narrative structure — the actual process is awaiting confirmation.

Understand the context → Define user needs → Map core journeys → Explore low-fidelity layouts → Refine mid-fidelity screens → Develop final UI → Prototype and evaluate.

Iteration focus

During the four-week project, I explored which service should lead the experience. After several iterations, I prioritized food pre-ordering and made reservations and technical support optional, aligning the main journey with the revenue priority of the café.

04 / Goals & Objectives

  • Make reserving a table or dedicated workspace intuitive.
  • Enable food and beverage ordering with minimal effort.
  • Make technical support easy to request when needed.
  • Reduce waiting through pre-ordering and reservations.
  • Support a productive and enjoyable café experience.

These are intended outcomes; measured results have not yet been provided.

05 / User Research

Target audience

Primary: IT professionals, software developers, UI/UX designers, freelancers, and remote workers.

Secondary: college students, startup teams, digital nomads, and entrepreneurs.

Research approach

I used AI tools for most of the research on Byte & Brew. This informed the exploration of the café-workspace concept. The audience profile and empathy map are working hypotheses, rather than findings from direct user interviews.

Research focus

The brief centers on seating availability, ordering delays, workspace amenities, and technical interruptions. These define the initial problem space.

Questions to validate

  • What information do users need before reserving a workspace?
  • How do they prefer to combine ordering with a reservation?
  • Which technical issues do they expect a café service to handle?
  • Can they understand the combined order and reservation costs?

The specific AI tools, source material, and any additional research activities are still to be documented. Direct user validation has not been confirmed.

The audience and pain points above reflect the project brief and AI-assisted exploration. They should be validated with prospective users.

11 / Challenges & Learnings

The challenge: deciding which journey comes first

Byte & Brew combines café ordering, workspace and table reservations, and technical support. My biggest challenge was deciding which service should lead the experience.

The decision: prioritize food pre-ordering

After several iterations, I chose food pre-ordering as the primary journey, based on the business rationale that food is the main revenue source for the café. Workspace and table reservations, along with technical support, became optional additions rather than required steps.

How the decision appears in the design

The cart offers both Reserve seat and Proceed to check out. The order summary offers Add Tech Support or Skip to Checkout. These choices let users continue with a food order while adding other services when relevant.

What I learned

A product with multiple services needs a clear hierarchy. Choosing a primary journey helped me organize the experience around the business priority while keeping the other services accessible and optional.

Next validation step

Test whether users can complete a food-only pre-order and discover reservations and support when needed. The effect of this hierarchy on usability or revenue has not yet been measured.

About

From infrastructure to UI/UX

“Why did you move from infrastructure to UI/UX?” is a question I’m often asked.

Working in infrastructure taught me how systems run and how to solve problems behind the scenes. Over time, I became curious about the people using those systems—how they felt, where they struggled, and what could make their experience easier.

During a break from IT, I explored teaching, creative work, and different projects. I didn’t always have a clear direction, and there were days when I questioned what I was doing. Looking back, that time gave me space to rediscover myself, learn new things, and understand what I wanted from my work.

When I was ready to return to technology, UI/UX design brought those interests together. It gave me a way to combine my technical background with my love of listening, understanding people, and solving everyday problems.

Being a mother to a neurodivergent child has also shaped this journey. It continues to teach me patience, flexibility, and how differently people experience the world—lessons I carry into the designer I’m becoming.

Outside of work, I cherish quiet time with books and favourite shows as much as I enjoy travelling and deep conversations. I’m a people person who values time to reflect. That balance of connection and curiosity is what draws me to design: the chance to make technology feel more understandable, welcoming, and useful.

Design Thinking

My Design Philosophy

As a UI/UX Product Designer, I believe good design starts with understanding people. Every product exists to solve a real problem. My role as a designer is to observe, question, and translate user needs into experiences that feel simple, intuitive, and efficient.

I focus on three core principles:

User Empathy

Understanding user behaviour, challenges, and motivations before making any design decisions.

Clarity over Complexity

Designing interfaces that feel natural and easy to navigate, especially for first-time users.

Purposeful Decisions

Every layout, colour, interaction, and component should support the user's goal — nothing arbitrary.

For me, product design is not just about creating screens — it is about designing experiences that make technology more accessible and useful for people.

Human thinking, supported by AI

I started learning design as the field was shifting into the AI era. There was excitement, but also uncertainty. Some people saw AI as the end of design careers; others saw new possibilities. As someone just starting out, I found that confusing.

Clarity came through learning and experimenting. Instead of trying to predict where AI would take the industry, I began exploring how it could support my own workflow. I found it useful for exploring research questions, developing ideas, prototyping, and working with design-system components.

I believe AI should support human thinking, not replace it. It can help me move faster, explore alternatives, and refine details—but I’m responsible for questioning its suggestions, checking its accuracy, and deciding what makes sense for the people I’m designing for. AI-generated assumptions are starting points to investigate, not substitutes for listening to users.

My workflow combines different tools depending on the task. I move between AI-assisted exploration, sketches, Figma, and code, reviewing and refining the work along the way. I’m learning how these tools can connect design with front-end development while keeping the experience consistent and purposeful.

For me, becoming a designer in the AI era means learning both how to use the tools and when to trust my own judgment. The tools will keep changing. My focus remains on understanding people and making thoughtful design decisions.

How I Explain UX to Non-Designers

I like to explain UX design with a simple analogy: imagine building a house.

An architect designs the blueprint — deciding where rooms should be, how people will move through the space, where doors and windows go. Engineers and builders construct the house. But the most important person is the one who will live in it — they have specific needs around how the kitchen is arranged, where parking should be, the direction of the balcony.

UX design works in a similar way. Designers create the blueprint for digital products — how the product is structured, how users move through it, and how easy it is to use. At every stage, the designer asks: "Does this make sense for the user?"

Because ultimately, good design is not about the product itself — it's about how people experience it.
